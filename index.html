CAN Bus Fundamentals

Understanding Controller Area Network - the communication backbone of modern elevator systems. Even if you've never worked with electronics, you'll understand how elevators "talk" to each other.

What is CAN Bus? (For Complete Beginners)

Controller Area Network (CAN) is a communication protocol that allows electronic devices to talk to each other. Think of it like a group conversation where everyone listens, but only the most important person speaks first.

Real-World Analogy: The Office Meeting

Imagine an office meeting with 10 people. In a normal meeting (like old wiring), everyone shouts at once and you need separate phone lines between every pair of people. With CAN bus, it's like having one conference call line where:

  • Everyone listens to the same line (all devices receive all messages)
  • The CEO (safety system) speaks first if there's an emergency
  • Everyone else waits their turn based on importance
  • If someone says something garbled, everyone knows to ask them to repeat

Why Elevators Use CAN Bus

Before CAN bus, elevators used dozens of individual wires between every device. A 20-floor elevator might need 200+ separate wires. With CAN bus, you need just 2 wires for all communication. This means:

  • Less wiring = Lower installation cost & less weight
  • Less weight = Smaller motor needed = Energy savings
  • Self-checking = Automatically detects broken wires or interference
  • Standardized = Any brand device can work with any other brand

How CAN Bus Actually Works (Non-Technical)

CAN bus uses two wires called CAN_HCAN_H (CAN High)
The positive voltage wire in the differential pair. Typically carries 2.5V-3.5V signals. and CAN_LCAN_L (CAN Low)
The negative voltage wire in the differential pair. Typically carries 1.5V-2.5V signals.. When a device wants to send a message, it changes the voltage on both wires simultaneously. Other devices "listen" by measuring the voltage difference.

Why Two Wires? (Differential Signaling)

Instead of measuring voltage on one wire compared to ground, CAN measures the difference between the two wires. This means if electrical noise affects both wires equally, the difference stays the same. It's like noise-canceling headphones for wires!

Dominant vs Recessive

Dominant (0): CAN_H goes HIGH, CAN_L goes LOW. This "wins" over recessive.

Recessive (1): Both wires sit at middle voltage. Only sent if no one else is talking.

Analogy: Dominant is like shouting - it overrides whispering. Safety messages always shout.

Arbitration (Who Talks First)

When two devices talk at once, the one with the LOWER ID number wins.

ID 0x001 (safety) beats ID 0x100 (display update).

Analogy: Like a hospital - the cardiac arrest alarm (ID 1) interrupts the lunch menu update (ID 100).

Common CAN Bus Faults & How to Fix Them

⚠️
Fault: No Communication - All Nodes Offline
The entire CAN bus is silent
▼

Symptoms

  • No heartbeat messages from any device
  • All status LEDs red or off
  • Emergency mode activated
1

Check Termination Resistors

Measure resistance between CAN_H and CAN_L with power OFF. Should read ~60Ω (two 120Ω resistors in parallel). If 0Ω = short circuit. If ∞Ω = open circuit/missing terminator.

Fix: Install 120Ω resistors at BOTH ends of the bus. Remove any resistors in the middle.

2

Verify Power Supply

All CAN transceivers need 5V or 3.3V power. Check voltage at the transceiver pins (not just the power supply output).

Fix: Check for loose connections, blown fuses, or overloaded power supplies.

3

Check for Reversed Wires

CAN_H and CAN_L are often swapped during installation. This causes the differential signal to be inverted.

Fix: Swap the two wires at one end only. Use a multimeter to identify which wire is which if color coding is unclear.

📡
Fault: Intermittent Communication
Messages sometimes lost or corrupted
▼

Symptoms

  • Random error frames on bus
  • Devices occasionally go into "Bus Off" state
  • Position jumps or display flickers
1

Check Cable Quality

CAN requires twisted pair cable with specific impedance (120Ω). Flat cable or untwisted wire picks up noise.

Fix: Use proper CAN bus cable (twisted pair, 0.25-0.5mm², shielded in noisy environments). Check for kinks or damage.

2

Verify Grounding

The CAN shield should be grounded at ONE end only (usually the controller end). Grounding both ends creates ground loops.

Fix: Ground shield at controller. Isolate at far end using RC network or leave floating.

3

Check Stub Lengths

Each device connects via a "stub" (short wire) to the main bus. Stubs longer than 0.3m cause signal reflections.

Fix: Keep stubs under 30cm. Use "daisy chain" wiring, not star topology.

This makes it perfect for elevators where safety-critical messages (like emergency stops) must never be lost.

Understanding the standards that make different elevator devices work together. Think of CiA profiles as "dictionaries" that ensure every device speaks the same language.

What is CiA? (For Non-Technical People)

CAN in Automation (CiA) is an international users' and manufacturers' group that creates standard "rulebooks" for how devices communicate over CAN bus. Without these standards, every elevator manufacturer would use different message formats, and you couldn't mix devices from different brands.

Analogy: The United Nations Translator

Imagine a UN meeting where everyone speaks a different language. CiA is like the team of translators who ensure everyone understands each other. They define:

  • Object Dictionary: A numbered list of every piece of data a device can share (like a phone book)
  • Message Types: What kind of envelope to use for different messages
  • Timing: How often to send updates and how long to wait before assuming something is wrong
  • Safety: Special rules for life-critical messages (like emergency stops)

How to Use the Object Dictionary

Reading (SDO Upload): Send "Tell me your value at address 0x2000" → Device replies "3000" (door open time is 3 seconds).

Writing (SDO Download): Send "Set address 0x2000 to 5000" → Device changes door open time to 5 seconds.

Why this matters: You can configure ANY CANopen device from ANY manufacturer using the same software tool, because they all use the same addressing scheme.

CiA 306 - Safety Communication Explained

Safety communication is the most critical part of elevator CAN bus. CiA 306 ensures that if a safety device says "STOP," the controller absolutely, positively receives that message - or knows something went wrong.

1

Sequence Counter

Every safety message has an incrementing number (1, 2, 3, 4...). If the receiver gets 1, 2, 4, it knows message 3 was lost.

Simple: Like counting "1, 2, 3..." - if you hear "1, 2, 5" you know something was missed! The system immediately goes to safe state.

2

CRC-32 Checksum

32-bit mathematical fingerprint of the entire message. Even one bit changed (due to electrical noise) = completely different checksum = message rejected.

Simple: Like a password hash - if the message changes even slightly, the "password" won't match. The system ignores corrupted messages.

3

Safety Timeout (Watchdog)

If no safety message arrives within the expected time (typically 50-200ms), the system assumes communication is broken and enters safe state.

Simple: Like a "dead man's switch" on a train - if the driver doesn't press the button regularly, the train stops. Silence = danger.

4

Dual-Channel Redundancy

Two independent CAN channels send the same safety message. Both must agree. If Channel A says "Safe" and Channel B says "Unsafe," the system goes safe.

Simple: Like having two judges - both must agree. Disagreement = safety. This prevents single points of failure.

PDO vs SDO - Real-Time vs Configuration

PDO (Process Data Object)

Speed: Fast, no confirmation

Use: Real-time data (position, speed, status)

Size: Up to 8 bytes

Analogy: Like a live TV broadcast - fast, one-way, no confirmation needed.

Example: Encoder sends position every 10ms via TPDO. Controller sends speed command via RPDO.

SDO (Service Data Object)

Speed: Slower, confirmed delivery

Use: Configuration, parameters, diagnostics

Size: Up to 4 bytes per message (segmented for larger)

Analogy: Like registered mail - slower, but you get a receipt confirming delivery.

Example: Technician reads door open time from Object Dictionary 0x2000 via SDO. Controller confirms receipt.

If car exceeds rated speed by > 15%, the governor must trigger within 50ms. Emergency brake must stop car within 1g deceleration.

Step-by-Step Implementation

1

Physical Layer Setup

Connect devices with shielded twisted pair. Install 120Ω termination resistors at both ends. Keep stub lengths under 30cm.

Pro Tip: Use a multimeter to verify 60Ω between CAN_H and CAN_L with power OFF. This confirms both terminators are present and no short circuits exist.

2

Assign Node IDs

Each device needs a unique Node ID (1-127). Document your ID assignment plan before powering up.

Best Practice: Reserve IDs 1-10 for safety-critical devices. Use 11-50 for standard I/O. Keep 51-127 for future expansion.

3

Configure Baud Rate

All devices must use the same baud rate. For elevator shafts, use 125 or 250 kbps.

Rule of Thumb: 125 kbps for shafts >50m. 250 kbps for standard buildings. 500 kbps only for machine room connections.

4

Configure PDO Mapping

Set up which data each device sends and receives. Map Object Dictionary entries to PDO slots.

Example: Map encoder position (0x6064) to TPDO1 bytes 0-3. Map VFD speed setpoint (0x60FF) to RPDO1.

5

Configure Heartbeat

Set heartbeat intervals (100-500ms) for failure detection. Set heartbeat timeout to 2-3x the interval.

Safety Note: Safety devices should heartbeat every 50-100ms. Non-safety devices can use 500ms to reduce bus load.

// CRC-32 implementation for CiA 306 safety messages
uint32_t calculateCRC32(const uint8_t* data, uint8_t len) {
  uint32_t crc = 0xFFFFFFFF;
  for (int i = 0; i < len; i++) {
    crc ^= data[i];
    for (int j = 0; j < 8; j++) {
      if (crc & 1) crc = (crc >> 1) ^ 0xEDB88320;
      else crc >>= 1;
    }
  }
  return crc ^ 0xFFFFFFFF;
}

// Validate incoming safety message
bool validateSafetyMessage(uint8_t* data, uint8_t len, 
                              uint8_t expectedSequence) {
  // Check sequence counter
  if (data[0] != expectedSequence) {
    triggerSafeState("Sequence counter mismatch");
    return false;
  }

  // Verify CRC-32 (last 4 bytes)
  uint32_t receivedCRC = 
    ((uint32_t)data[len-4] << 24) |
    ((uint32_t)data[len-3] << 16) |
    ((uint32_t)data[len-2] << 8) |
    data[len-1];

  uint32_t calculatedCRC = calculateCRC32(data, len-4);

  if (receivedCRC != calculatedCRC) {
    triggerSafeState("CRC-32 mismatch - corrupted safety message");
    return false;
  }

  return true;
}

Process Data Objects (PDOs) carry real-time data with minimal overhead. Proper PDO mapping is critical for elevator performance — a misconfigured PDO can add 50+ ms of latency.

PDO Mapping Rules

  • Max 8 bytes per PDO — standard CAN data limit. If you need more data, use multiple PDOs or CAN FD.
  • Map related data together — position + velocity in one PDO, door status + load in another. Reduces bus load.
  • Use inhibit time — prevents a noisy analog input from flooding the bus. 1–10 ms is typical.
  • Event timer vs sync — Event timer = periodic (good for position). Sync = triggered (good for coordinated sampling). Use sync for multi-axis systems.
  • Total PDOs — CiA 301 allows 4 TPDOs and 4 RPDOs per node. Most elevator devices use 1–2 of each.

    {DOOR_STATE_CLOSING, EVENT_OBSTRUCTION, DOOR_STATE_OBSTRUCTED, actionReverseMotor, guardObstructionValid},
    {DOOR_STATE_CLOSING, EVENT_TIMEOUT, DOOR_STATE_FAULT, actionSetFault, guardTimeoutExceeded},

    // From OBSTRUCTED
    {DOOR_STATE_OBSTRUCTED, EVENT_CLEAR, DOOR_STATE_OPEN, actionHoldOpen, guardObstructionCleared},
    {DOOR_STATE_OBSTRUCTED, EVENT_TIMEOUT, DOOR_STATE_FAULT, actionSetFault, guardTimeoutExceeded},

    // From FAULT
    {DOOR_STATE_FAULT, EVENT_RESET, DOOR_STATE_CLOSED, actionResetFault, guardResetValid},
};

// Process state machine every 10 ms
void processDoorStateMachine(Door_Controller_t* door, uint32_t events) {
    for (int i = 0; i < sizeof(doorTransitions)/sizeof(doorTransitions[0]); i++) {
        State_Transition_t* trans = &doorTransitions[i];
        if (trans->currentState == door->state && 
            (events & trans->eventMask) &&
            trans->guard()) {
            door->state = trans->nextState;
            if (trans->action) trans->action();
            break;  // Only one transition per cycle
        }
    }
}

State Machine Best Practices

  • Always have a FAULT state — every state machine needs a safe harbor. From FAULT, only a deliberate reset action can resume.
  • Guard conditions prevent invalid transitions — e.g., can't transition from CLOSING to OPEN without passing through OBSTRUCTED if an object is detected.
  • Actions are idempotent — calling an action multiple times should not cause harm. This prevents race conditions.
  • Log all transitions — for debugging, log every state change with timestamp, old state, new state, and triggering event.
  • Timeout guards prevent deadlock — if a transition takes longer than expected, force a FAULT state.

Boot-Up Sequence & Network Discovery

When a CANopen elevator network powers on, devices must boot in the correct order and discover each other. A misconfigured boot sequence can leave safety devices offline.

1

Power-On Self-Test (POST)

Each node performs hardware checks: RAM test, flash CRC, CAN transceiver loopback, and sensor calibration. Takes 50–500 ms depending on node complexity.

What you see: Status LED blinks rapidly during POST, then changes pattern.

2

Boot-Up Message

After successful POST, each node sends a single boot-up message: COB-ID 0x700+NodeID, data = 0x00. This announces "I'm alive and ready."

Code: CAN.send(0x705, [0x00]); for Node 0x05. The master listens for these to build a node list.

3

Pre-Operational State

All nodes enter Pre-Operational automatically after boot-up. PDOs are disabled. SDO communication is active for configuration.

Why: This prevents devices from sending process data before they're fully configured. Like a pilot running through the pre-flight checklist before takeoff.

4

Master Configuration Phase

The master (Node 0x01) reads each node's Object Dictionary to verify identity, then writes configuration parameters (PDO mappings, heartbeat times, baud rate).

Duration: 1–5 seconds for a 10-node network. Longer if firmware updates are needed.

5

Transition to Operational

Master sends NMT Start (0x01, 0x00) to all nodes. All devices begin sending PDOs and heartbeats. The elevator is ready to run.

Verification: Monitor bus load — should jump from ~5% (boot-up only) to 30–50% (full operation).

6

Continuous Monitoring

Master monitors all heartbeats. If any node misses its deadline, the master initiates safe stop. Nodes monitor the master's heartbeat too — bidirectional safety.

Fail-safe: If the master fails, nodes detect missing NMT/heartbeat and enter Pre-Operational automatically within 3× heartbeat period.

// Master boot-up sequence implementation
#define MAX_NODES 32
#define BOOT_TIMEOUT_MS 5000

        nodeTable[i].isOperational = false;

    if (nodeId > 0 && nodeId <= MAX_NODES) {

        node->bootTimestamp = millis();

        // Read device identity via SDO

    }
}

    for (int i = 0; i < MAX_NODES; i++) {
        if (nodeTable[i].isPresent && !nodeTable[i].isOperational) {
            return false;
        }
    }
    return true;
}

void startOperationalPhase() {
    if (allNodesBooted()) {
        // Send NMT Start to all nodes
        CAN.send(0x000, [0x01, 0x00]);
        Serial.println("All nodes operational. Elevator ready.");

        // Start application state machines
        enableSafetyMonitoring();
        enablePositionControl();
    } else {
        Serial.println("ERROR: Not all nodes booted. Cannot start.");
        enterSafeState("Incomplete boot-up");
    }
}

// Call every 100 ms
void checkNodeHealth() {
    uint32_t now = millis();
    for (int i = 0; i < MAX_NODES; i++) {
        Node_Status_t* node = &nodeTable[i];
        if (node->isPresent && !node->isOperational) {
            if (now - node->bootTimestamp > BOOT_TIMEOUT_MS) {
                enterSafeState("Boot timeout for node " + node->nodeId);
            }
        }
        if (node->isOperational) {
            if (now - node->lastHeartbeat > HEARTBEAT_TIMEOUT_MS) {
                enterSafeState("Heartbeat timeout for node " + node->nodeId);
            }
        }
    }
}

EMC/EMI Considerations for Elevator CAN Bus

Elevators are electrically noisy environments. VFDs, motors, and contactors generate significant electromagnetic interference. Here's how to ensure reliable communication:

Cable Selection

  • Use shielded twisted pair with 120Ω characteristic impedance
  • Minimum wire gauge: 0.25mm² (24 AWG)
  • Maximum loop resistance: 110Ω/km
  • Recommended: CAN bus specific cable (e.g., Lapp Kabel UNITRONIC BUS)

Grounding & Shielding

  • Ground shield at ONE end only (controller end)
  • Use RC network (4.7nF + 1MΩ) at far end if needed
  • Keep CAN cable >30cm from VFD power cables
  • Use metal conduit for additional shielding in high-EMI areas

Exercise 1: Verify CAN Bus Termination

Incorrect termination is the #1 cause of CAN bus failures in the field. This 2-minute procedure eliminates guesswork.

1

Power Down All Nodes

Turn off power to every device on the CAN bus. You must measure resistance on a dead bus.

2

Set Multimeter to Ohms

Use a calibrated digital multimeter (e.g., Fluke 87V). Select the resistance (Ω) range.

3

Measure CAN_H to CAN_L

Place probes on CAN_H and CAN_L at any accessible point. Expected: ~60 Ω (two 120 Ω resistors in parallel).

Interpretation: 60 Ω = perfect. 120 Ω = one missing terminator. ∞ Ω = both missing or cable break. < 50 Ω = possible short or too many terminators.

4

Document & Resolve

Record the reading in your test log. Install or remove terminators as needed until you read 60 Ω.

Exercise 2: Configure a Replacement Device via LSS

When a CANopen node fails in the field, the replacement must assume the same Node ID. LSS lets you do this over the bus without opening enclosures.

1

Connect & Power the Replacement

Install the new device on the CAN bus and power it on. It will boot with Node ID 0 or factory default.

2

Run LSS Fastscan

Using your CAN analyzer (Vector/Kvaser), send LSS Fastscan to discover the unconfigured device. Note its LSS address (Vendor ID, Product Code, Revision, Serial).

3

Select & Configure Node ID

Use LSS Switch State Selective to target the device, then send LSS Configure Node-ID with the ID of the failed unit (e.g., 0x05).

4

Store & Reset

Send LSS Store Configuration to write to non-volatile memory. Then issue NMT Reset Node. Verify the device boots with the new Node ID and responds to SDO.

Pro Tip

Always keep a backup of your network's Node ID assignment sheet and EDS files on your laptop. This turns a 2-hour field visit into a 15-minute fix.

Exercise 3: Read Encoder Position via SDO

Verify that a CiA 406 encoder is reporting valid position data before commissioning the elevator.

SDO Upload Request (CiA 406 Position)
// Request: Read Object 0x6004 (Position Value) from Node 0x05
// COB-ID: 0x605 (0x600 + Node ID)
// Command: 0x40 = Initiate Upload (expedited)
CAN.send(0x605, [0x40, 0x04, 0x60, 0x00, 0x00, 0x00, 0x00, 0x00]);

// Expected Response from Node 0x05 on COB-ID 0x585:
// Byte 0: 0x43 (Expedited Upload, 4 bytes)
// Bytes 1-2: 0x04 0x60 (Index 0x6004, little-endian)
// Byte 3: 0x00 (Sub-index)
// Bytes 4-7: Position value as 32-bit signed integer (little-endian)
1

Verify Response

Confirm the response COB-ID is 0x585 (0x580 + 0x05). The first byte should be 0x43, confirming a 4-byte expedited upload.

2

Decode Position

Combine bytes 4-7 as a little-endian 32-bit signed integer. Example: [0xA0, 0x86, 0x01, 0x00] = 100,000 counts.

3

Physical Verification

Rotate the encoder shaft slowly. The position value should increment or decrement smoothly. Large jumps indicate coupling slippage or sensor errors.

Case Study 1: Erratic Position & Leveling Errors

Symptom

Elevator controller reports incorrect car position. Floor display jumps randomly. Doors occasionally open mid-shaft.

1

Investigation: Monitor Encoder PDO

Using a CAN analyzer, observe TPDO1 from the encoder (Node 0x05). Position values jump by thousands of counts intermittently.

2

Diagnosis: Read Error Registers

SDO read of 0x1001 (Error Register) shows bit 0 set. SDO read of 0x6503 (Alarm) shows sensor error bit active. Emergency messages (EMCY) correlate with jumps.

3

Root Cause: Mechanical Slippage

Physical inspection reveals the encoder coupling set screws are loose. The shaft spins intermittently without rotating the encoder disc.

4

Resolution

Tighten coupling set screws and verify with torque screwdriver. Re-verify position smoothness via SDO. Alarm clears. No further EMCY messages.

Lesson Learned

Always verify mechanical coupling integrity before suspecting electrical faults. A loose coupling produces symptoms identical to a broken encoder.

Case Study 2: Intermittent Communication During Peak Hours

Symptom

Random communication failures occur more frequently when multiple elevators run simultaneously. Position display flickers. Sporadic fault codes appear.

1

Investigation: Bus Analysis

CAN analyzer shows 5-10 error frames per minute. Oscilloscope reveals 200-300 mV noise spikes on CAN_H and CAN_L.

2

Diagnosis: EMI from VFD

Trace cable routing: a 2-meter section of CAN cable runs parallel to VFD motor output cables in the machine room. When multiple VFDs run, noise spikes increase.

3

Resolution: Reroute & Shield

Reroute CAN bus to maintain >30 cm separation from VFD power cables. Verify shield is grounded at controller end only. Noise drops to <50 mV. Error frames eliminated.

Lesson Learned

EMI is distance-dependent. Even a short parallel run of 1-2 meters can corrupt an otherwise healthy CAN bus. Always verify cable routing during commissioning.
